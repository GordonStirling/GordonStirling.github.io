Adding Value as a Data Practitioner / 02

OUTPUT → INFLUENCE → VALUE

This is part 2 of the Adding Value series, exploring how data practitioners create value in the real world.

What distinguishes an output that just exists from one that adds value?

Value is hard to measure. Isolating and quantifying how much any one intervention contributed to a performance outcome is extremely difficult. This is because outcomes emerge from complex systems made up of multiple elements, interconnections and purposes, all interacting in nonlinear and often unpredictable ways (Meadows, 2008).

We can rarely attribute a positive outcome to a single output, decision or action. Equally, a poor outcome does not necessarily mean that the analysis added no value.

In other words… it’s complicated.

We may not be able to precisely measure the value we add as data practitioners, but we can assess the influence our outputs have. As data practitioners, we add value through the positive influence our outputs have on the people, decisions and actions they are designed to support (Schimel, 2012). Doing this helps us identify what is working, where our work is getting stuck and what may need to change.

Influence is the mechanism through which our outputs add value.

So… we can distinguish between an output that just exists and one that adds value by assessing the influence it has on understanding, judgements, decisions or actions.

Output → Influence → Value

The “Levels of Influence” model (below) provides a practical way of assessing how far an output travels from delivery into practice. Some outputs may not get passed the Delivered stage, others may become Integrated. It is not a perfect science, adding value rarely is. But it gives us a simple language for describing the influence our outputs achieve.

The bottom two levels, Delivered and Consumed, are spaces I am all too familiar with. You send a report into a WhatsApp group and at best you get a thumbs up emoji in return… This is when you have produced an output but there is no evidence of influence from it. If an output repeatedly stalls at these levels, the feedback is clear… something needs to change. We will get into what that might be in a later post.

Demonstrated Influence begins at the Developed level. This is when an output strengthens, deepens, challenges or reframes someone’s understanding. It may increase their confidence in an existing belief, help them explain an outcome in more detail or introduce a different interpretation. To create influence you don’t have to change someone’s mind. Reducing uncertainty or improving clarity can deepen understanding, this is influence.

The Applied level is reached when the understanding developed through the analysis is used to inform a judgement, decision or action. The output has moved beyond deepening someone’s understanding and is now shaping what they decide or how they act. This may not always be visible; the application of the output may inform a deliberate decision not to act.

At the highest level, analytical practice becomes Integrated, this represents a shift from occasional influence to systemic influence. Analytical practice becomes embedded in routine workflows, repeatedly and reliably informing judgements, decisions and actions.

Integrated influence doesn’t happen overnight, no single output creates integration. It develops through what Jim Collins described as the flywheel effect. Relevant outputs encourage use, use increases utility, utility builds trust, trust brings analysis into conversations earlier. This creates greater context which enables increasingly relevant outputs. Each turn builds momentum for the next… the flywheel turns faster and faster driving more application of increasingly effective and efficient analysis.

Eventually, analysis is no longer simply existing within the environment or being pushed into conversations. Instead, it gets pulled into the workflows and conversations where judgements are formed, decisions are made and actions are planned.
